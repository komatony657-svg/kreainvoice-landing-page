How to Invoice Photography Clients as a Freelancer in Uganda

A step-by-step guide to creating professional photography invoices in Uganda — UGX currency, mobile money payment notes, and client-ready PDF templates.

Getting paid as a freelance photographer in Uganda comes with its own specific challenges — clients who want to negotiate after delivery, mobile money payments that need to be specified clearly, and invoices that need to show UGX amounts without looking like they were made in Microsoft Word 2007.

This guide covers everything you need to know about creating professional photography invoices in Uganda — from the right currency format to including mobile money payment details.

Why your invoice format matters in Uganda

Most Ugandan creative freelancers send invoices as WhatsApp messages, rough Word documents, or worse — verbal agreements. This creates three problems: clients forget what they agreed to pay, disputes arise over scope, and you look less professional than your work deserves.

A clean, detailed invoice signals that you run a real business. It makes clients take payment seriously. And it protects you when someone claims they "didn't know" the deposit was non-refundable.

What to include on a photography invoice in Uganda

Your studio details

Your full name or studio name, email address, location (city is enough — you don't need a full address), and your portfolio URL or Instagram handle. This establishes who the invoice is from and gives the client a way to verify your identity.

Client details

The client's name or company name, their email, and their location. For corporate clients, get the billing contact name specifically — invoices sent to a general company email often get lost.

Invoice number

Use a consistent format. Something like KS-2026-001 (your initials, year, sequence number) looks intentional and makes it easy to reference in follow-up conversations. Kreainvoice auto-increments after each PDF download so you never repeat a number.

Issue date and due date

Always include both. In Uganda, "pay me when you can" is not a payment term. Write the exact due date — 7 days, 14 days, or 30 days from the issue date. 14 days is standard for most photography work.

Currency — UGX

Always invoice in Uganda Shillings for local clients unless the client specifically requested a different currency. UGX amounts should be written clearly — UGX 550,000 not "550k" or "half a million". Kreainvoice supports UGX natively in the currency dropdown.

Line items with clear descriptions

Don't write "Photography — UGX 500,000" as a single line. Break it down:

  • Photography coverage — 1 day: UGX 300,000
  • Post-processing — 50 images: UGX 150,000
  • Usage rights — web, Uganda, 12 months: UGX 50,000

This shows the client exactly what they're paying for and makes it harder to dispute individual items.

Payment details — mobile money

This is the most Uganda-specific part. Always include your mobile money details in the notes section of your invoice. Be specific:

Payment via MTN Mobile Money: 0771 234 567 (Your Name)
Payment via Airtel Money: 0701 234 567 (Your Name)
Payment due within 14 days of invoice date.

If you accept bank transfer, include your bank name, account name, and account number. The less friction between the client and paying you, the faster you get paid.

Deposits and milestone invoicing

For any job over UGX 200,000, always invoice a deposit upfront — typically 30-50% of the total. This covers your time if the client cancels and filters out clients who were never serious about paying.

Structure it as two separate invoices:

  • Invoice 1 (deposit): 50% due before the shoot date
  • Invoice 2 (balance): 50% due within 7 days of delivery

Usage rights for Ugandan photographers

Always specify usage rights on your invoice, even for small jobs. A product shoot for a local brand might seem straightforward, but if they later use the images on billboards or in a national TV campaign, you have no basis to charge extra without a written licence.

For most local commercial work, a simple clause works: "Web and print use, Uganda only, 12 months, non-exclusive." Anything beyond that scope should be a separate conversation — and a separate invoice.

Following up on unpaid invoices

Send a polite reminder on the due date if payment hasn't arrived. Wait three days, then send a firmer follow-up referencing the invoice number. After a week, make a phone call — in Uganda, a direct call often resolves things faster than messages.

Having a clear invoice with a specific due date and payment method gives you solid ground to stand on in these conversations. "The invoice says payment was due on the 27th via MTN Mobile Money" is harder to argue with than "you said you'd pay me."
