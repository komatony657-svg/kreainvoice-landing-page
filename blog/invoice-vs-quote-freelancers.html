Invoice vs Quote — What's the Difference for Freelancers?

Quotes win clients. Invoices get you paid. Here's when to send each, what to include, and how to turn a quote into an invoice in one click.

If you've ever sent a price breakdown to a client before the work started, you sent a quote. If you've ever asked to be paid after doing the work, you sent an invoice. They look similar but they serve completely different purposes — and confusing the two can cost you clients and payments.

The one-sentence difference

A quote is an offer. A invoice is a demand for payment. A quote says "here's what I'll charge if you hire me." An invoice says "you hired me, the work is done, please pay this amount by this date."

What is a quote?

A quote (sometimes called a proposal or estimate) is a document you send to a potential client before any work begins. It outlines what you'll deliver, how much it will cost, and what the terms are. The client can accept it, negotiate it, or decline it.

A good quote includes:

  • A clear scope of work — exactly what you're delivering
  • Itemised pricing for each deliverable
  • A valid-until date (quotes shouldn't be open-ended)
  • Payment terms that will apply if accepted
  • Any conditions — number of revisions, deposit required, etc.

Quotes are not legally binding until the client accepts them. Once accepted — in writing, ideally — they form the basis of your agreement.

What is an invoice?

An invoice is a formal request for payment after work has been completed or at a milestone. It references the agreed scope and price, adds a due date, and specifies how to pay. Unlike a quote, an invoice creates a legal obligation to pay.

A good invoice includes:

  • Invoice number and date
  • Due date with specific payment terms
  • Itemised line items matching the agreed scope
  • Payment details (mobile money, bank transfer)
  • Usage rights if applicable

💡 Always reference the original quote on your invoice. Something like "As per quote QT-2026-004 dated 15 May 2026" gives both parties a clear paper trail and reduces disputes.

When to send a quote vs an invoice

Send a quote: when a client asks for your rates, when you're pitching for a new project, when scope needs to be agreed before work starts, or when a client wants to compare options.

Send an invoice: after a quote has been accepted, when work is complete or at an agreed milestone, when a deposit is due, or when a retainer period ends.

The quote-to-invoice workflow

The most professional workflow goes like this:

  1. Client enquires about your services
  2. You send a detailed quote with a valid-until date
  3. Client accepts the quote in writing (email is fine)
  4. You send a deposit invoice — typically 30-50%
  5. Client pays deposit, work begins
  6. Work is delivered
  7. You send a final invoice for the remaining balance
  8. Client pays, project closes

This process protects both parties. The client knows exactly what they're getting and what it costs. You have written confirmation before spending time on the project.

Common mistakes to avoid

Sending an invoice without a quote

Jumping straight to an invoice without agreeing on scope first is one of the most common causes of payment disputes. The client expected one thing, you delivered another, and neither of you has written proof of what was agreed.

Open-ended quotes

A quote without a valid-until date can be accepted months later at the old price. Always add a validity period — two to four weeks is standard.

Invoicing before delivery

With the exception of deposits, send your invoice when the work is done — not before. An invoice for undelivered work creates resentment and payment resistance.
